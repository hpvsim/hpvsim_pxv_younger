# Reviewer 1 comments

This study derives an equation linking the vaccine efficacy and required vaccination coverage between two vaccination strategies—adolescent vaccination (ages 9–14) and infant vaccination—to achieve theoretically equivalent health outcomes. An agent-based model of HPV transmission and cervical disease was employed to simulate the effects of these strategies under specific parameter settings. The authors stated that this research aims to encourage evidence generation and accumulation on the safety, efficacy, and durability of administering the HPV vaccine to neonates or toddlers. However, the study does not sufficiently quantify the potential benefits and feasibility of an infant HPV vaccination program. Additionally, the analysis presented does not effectively support its stated research objective. Several major concerns remain:

1.In the Methods section, the authors state: "Our scenarios assumed that the infant vaccine program would be able to attain coverage levels of 90%." The rationale for this assumption should be further explained to support its reasonableness.

2.In the Results section, the authors state: "We can use the effective coverage equivalency in Equation 1 to calculate the vaccine efficacy for infants required to obtain the same health outcomes as a strategy of vaccinating adolescents." However, Equation 1 merely ensures theoretical comparability between the two sides of the equation. As demonstrated in the results, the simulated outcomes for the corresponding parameters are not entirely identical. Consequently, the term 'same' is misleading and should be modified. Furthermore, the specific health outcomes evaluated should be explicitly defined in the Methods section.

3.The results presented in Figure 3 do not appear to provide substantial added value to the study. First, the vaccine efficacy required for infant vaccination to match health outcomes of adolescent vaccination (assuming 95% efficacy for adolescents and 90% coverage for infants) has already been addressed in the first part of the results and could be more efficiently conveyed via a reference line in Figure 1 with accompanying text. Furthermore, given the assumption that Equation 1 calculates equivalent effective coverage between the two strategies, the similarity between the left and right panels of Figure 3 is inherently predictable. If the authors wish to retain these results, they should clarify their unique analytical contribution to justify their inclusion.

4.The discussion lacks sufficient depth. If the authors intend to advocate for further research on the immunogenicity and safety of HPV vaccination in infants, they must provide a robust justification for the advantages and feasibility of this approach. Specifically, based on the study’s findings, the discussion should explore whether the required coverage for infant vaccination to achieve similar outcomes as the existing adolescent vaccination strategy is realistically attainable. Additionally, a balanced assessment of the economic costs, implementation barriers, and potential public health benefits is essential to determine whether this strategy offers meaningful advantages over existing protocols. All key claims should be supported by robust evidence from prior studies or empirical data.

Additionally, a few minor concerns should be addressed:

1.In the Methods section, under Simulation analyses, the authors state:"To estimate the potential health impact of the infant vaccine program, we ran simulations with varying levels of coverage for 9–14-year-old girls (10–90%), as well as the efficacy of the infant vaccine at the point of sexual debut (50–90%), limiting our scope to this range since efficacy values lower than this would be unlikely to be approved." When considering vaccination for the 9–14 age group, how is coverage distributed across different ages? Does cumulative coverage change over time for each age group? The vaccination mode should be explicitly described. Additionally, it is recommended that the authors provide information on HPV infection prevalence and the distribution of sexual debut among the 9–14-year-old population in Nigeria to enhance readers' understanding of the model simulation settings.

2.In the Results section, the authors state: "Under this assumption, the health impact of the infant vaccination strategy would be at least as high as the adolescent vaccination strategy as long as the efficacy of the infant vaccine is reasonably high. However, at lower levels of efficacy..." The terms "high" and "lower" are vague. Please specify clear thresholds or numerical ranges for these terms.

3.In the Discussion section, the authors state: "We found, for example, that an infant vaccine would need to achieve effective coverage of at least 80% in order to have a similar health impact to Nigeria’s current adolescent vaccination program." This number should be verified, as there is no corresponding statement in the Results section.

# Reviewer 2 comments

This is an interesting paper with potential to influence HPV vaccination policies across Africa. I have a few questions/concerns for your consideration.  
1\. The main assumptions in the paper revolve around stable immunity in infants compared to girls 9-14 years. Citations 8 \-12 are used to justify claims around “durable protection against cervical cancer for at least 10 years when administered to girls.” Looking at these citations, reference 8 has a median follow-up of 11.3 years, but looks at 18-25-year-old bivalent vaccine recipients. Reference 9 looks at cross-protection. Reference 12 looks at 15-20-year-olds assessed at 18 months post-vaccination. Similar issues for the other references.  
2\. That is, these citations either have different populations, or different study timelines, or different endpoints e.g., persistent HPV infection and not cervical cancer. That is, you are making claims around heterogeneous studies and using this to justify your model in terms of durability of effect. We need more information about this.  
3\. You mention parameters for the model, but these are not presented in a table format with their distributions to allow for critical review. You need to present this table in the classical table 1 of assumptions format to allow the reader to evaluate the appropriateness of these parameters.  
4\. Stemming from the preceding point – you could tell us how you handled stochastic variability or provide uncertainty intervals around the estimates, especially for your base case. Also mention how you conducted these uncertainty analyses.  
5\. Also add an explicit sentence in the methods for what your base case assumptions are.  
6\. I am uncomfortable with the 90% infant coverage assumption. A quick look online shows that the DTP3 coverage in Nigeria in 2023 was around 62%. There is of course geographical heterogeneity, but you need to use this value as your baseline. This means your results are ex ante optimistic.  
7\. Add some background information on the Nigeria HPV strategy. What is the schedule? How many doses are offered – single or multiple? Is there any geographical heterogeneity in vaccine uptake? Is there any geographical heterogeneity in cervical cancer?  
8\. I also noticed online that the Nigeria regulatory approval was for quadrivalent vaccine, but you model a 9-valent vaccine. You need to support this.  
9\. Try and tie the 10-year non-waning immunity to years to sexual debut and beyond. That is, for infants, the question should be the shape of waning over time. You therefore need to model different waning scenarios – not just scalar endpoints. That is, change this from a threshold model to a waning scenario to increase biological plausibility.  
10\. I am also worried about you making long horizon predictions – up to 2100, without giving us detailed data on other things that may influence your numbers like demographic projections. For example, Nigeria’s population is projected to more than double by 2100\. How does the model handle this?  
11\. Minor: why do you use 30% adolescent coverage when current coverage is 85% as you report?  
12\. Minor: could you also provide a couple of sentences describing the model – even if this has been described in detail elsewhere.

# Reviewer 3 comments

This manuscript addresses a highly relevant and timely public health topic. The evaluation of prophylactic HPV vaccination strategies in infants or very young children is an innovative and important area of research, particularly in high-burden settings such as Nigeria. The study has the potential to contribute meaningfully to ongoing discussions about optimizing HPV prevention strategies globally. However, the manuscript in its current form requires substantial revision to fully realize its scientific value and improve clarity, structure, and rigor.

General Comments  
The topic is compelling and of clear importance. The modeling approach is appropriate in principle, and the focus on Nigeria strengthens the manuscript’s relevance. Nevertheless, issues with writing style, organization, methodological clarity, and interpretation of results limit the manuscript’s current impact. A major revision is recommended.

Specific Comments  
Abstract  
1\. The Methods section should consistently use the past tense, as it describes completed work.  
2\. More broadly, there is a lack of consistency in tense usage throughout the manuscript, including the abstract. This should be corrected to align with standard academic writing conventions.

Introduction  
1\. The Introduction is overly detailed and contains content that would be more appropriate for the Discussion section.  
2\. There are repetitions that should be removed to improve readability and flow. Consider replacing the term “sexual debut” with “sexual initiation” for greater precision and formality.  
3\. To provide context for the modeling outputs, the authors should explicitly state that Nigeria has a population exceeding 240 million, which would help readers better interpret the scale and implications of the results.

Methods  
1\. The manuscript refers to a simulation analysis, but does not sufficiently present or justify the underlying data and assumptions. Additional detail is needed to support the modeling framework.  
2\. The authors should provide evidence or references validating key assumptions used in the model. The statement regarding treatment effectiveness should be corrected or supported:  
Excisional procedures are generally more effective at viral clearance than ablative treatments. This distinction should be accurately reflected in the model assumptions or discussed appropriately.

Results  
1\. The figures are not self-explanatory and are difficult to interpret in their current form. Abbreviations such as “VCA \= 100” or “VCA \= 90” are not clearly defined.  
2\. All figure labels and legends should be fully explained. Figures should be revised to ensure they are clear, interpretable without excessive reference to the main text, and aligned with standard publication quality.

Discussion  
1\. It repeats results unnecessarily, rather than interpreting them.  
2\. It does not adequately compare findings with existing literature. The authors should engage more deeply with similar modeling studies or HPV vaccination research, highlighting agreements, discrepancies, and the added value of their work. The implications of infant HPV vaccination should be discussed more critically, including feasibility, ethics, and programmatic considerations.

# Reviewer 4 comments

The manuscript by Stuart et al. investigates the potential effects of vaccination in a younger cohort, using a modeling framework to explore alternative HPV vaccination delivery strategies. Although the topic is interesting and the generation of such data would be valuable, the manuscript requires several revisions before it can be considered for publication.

• The results section of the abstract would benefit from clarification and tightening to ensure that the key findings and their interpretation are clearly conveyed and not overstated.

• Please be cautious when referring to HPV as an STI. While HPV is transmitted through sexual contact, the framing should be precise and consistent with public health and vaccination literature to avoid unnecessary stigma or oversimplification.

• The manuscript should more clearly distinguish the rationale for broadening the upper age limit of HPV vaccination (second paragraph introduction). This strategy has primarily been implemented for catch‑up vaccination, targeting individuals who were already older than the recommended age when the vaccine was introduced. This differs conceptually from the scenario explored here, which considers a shift toward childhood or infant vaccination, and should not be conflated with vaccinating at older ages.

• The introduction currently contains a substantial amount of material that would be more appropriate for the discussion section. Both the introduction and discussion would benefit from the inclusion of additional references to better support key statements and assumptions.

• It is unclear whether the analysis accounts for fundamental differences in immune system maturity between infants and young girls aged 9–14 years. Can current knowledge regarding immune response and waning of protection be directly extrapolated to infants? In addition, existing long‑term studies provide follow‑up over a limited number of years (\<12y), which aligns with protection needs when vaccination occurs closer to the age of HPV exposure. In contrast, infant vaccination would require protection over a much longer interval before peak incidence. The manuscript should address whether a single‑dose schedule would be sufficient in this population, and whether there is any empirical or immunological evidence to support this assumption.

• The scenarios assume that an infant HPV vaccination program could achieve coverage levels of 90%. The feasibility of attaining such coverage should be discussed in greater detail. Additionally, all numerical assumptions and estimates (e.g., coverage rates, cancer incidence, cancer cases averted) should be consistently supported by appropriate references where possible.

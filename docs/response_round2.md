**Comment 1**: Please provide additional sensitivity analyses around the assumed education-related vaccination and screening uptake differences, as these assumptions are key drivers of the equity-related conclusions.

**Response**: We agree that the education-uptake assumptions are central to the equity conclusions, and the revised manuscript addresses this on two levels. The model carries two education-linked odds ratios — one for vaccination and one for screening — each applied between primary-school-completers and non-completers. The vaccination OR of 5 is drawn from in-school vs. out-of-school HPV campaign coverage gaps reported for Malawi and Ethiopia (edu_OR ≈ 5–12; refs 29, 30), as no study has yet measured this gap for Nigeria's own HPV program; the screening OR of 3 is estimated directly from a Nigerian population-based study of women aged 30–49 (ref 33).

The sensitivity is built into the scenario matrix itself (Table 1). Each OR is toggled between its baseline value and 1 (no education gap) in a paired comparison. The **screening OR (3 vs. 1)** contrast is isolated by running the screening scale-up scenario twice, holding vaccination fixed at the status-quo 60% with vax_OR = 5; the equitable variant averts approximately 6–8% more cancers in vaccine-targetable cohorts and 5–7% more in the pre-2015 cohort. The **vaccination OR (5 vs. 1)** contrast is isolated by comparing the status-quo scenario (60% aggregate, OR = 5) with the WHO scale-up scenario (90% aggregate, OR = 1) and with the infant delivery scenarios (which assume uniform coverage across the birth cohort). The WHO scenario deliberately couples aggregate coverage lift and gap closure, on the grounds that reaching 90% aggregate coverage in Nigeria is only achievable if the current in-school/out-of-school gap is closed.

In response to the reviewer, we also ran a finer sweep across intermediate OR values, holding all other parameters at their baseline: vax_OR ∈ {2, 3, 8} at status-quo 60% aggregate coverage and 15% baseline screening, and screening_OR ∈ {2, 5} at the 70% screening scale-up target with status-quo vaccination (Supplementary Figure S4 and Supplementary Table S3). Two findings emerge:

- The **vaccination OR sweep** produces essentially null differences in the total burden in vaccine-targetable cohorts (2025–2100), with all four OR values (2, 3, 5, 8) landing within a ±15K envelope of each other and 95% UIs spanning zero (see Fig S4A). This is a scientifically clean result: at a fixed 60% aggregate coverage, changing the OR only redistributes who is vaccinated (in-school vs. out-of-school), not the total number vaccinated, so the aggregate burden estimate is insensitive to the OR assumption. The vax_OR value shapes the *distribution* of residual burden across education groups (which is what the paper's equity paragraph already discusses); it does not shift the aggregate.
- The **screening OR sweep** shows a monotonic and near-linear decline in cancers averted as the OR increases: at 70% aggregate scale-up, cancers averted in vaccine-targetable cohorts (2025–2100) move from ~153K (OR = 1, no gap; IQR 121–190K; 95% UI 71–222K) → ~141K (OR = 2) → ~127K (OR = 3, baseline; IQR 101–151K) → ~117K (OR = 5, larger gap than we assume). The direction of the finding — that screening scale-up substantially reduces residual burden but its magnitude depends on how equitably it reaches non-primary-completers — is unchanged across the tested range.

Both findings support the qualitative conclusion of the paper: residual burden concentrates on cohorts with weaker educational attainment, and infant delivery only resolves this if the routine platform is more equitable than school-based delivery.

We also revised the Discussion §Limitations paragraph to name the OR values as single-point assumptions from regional (vaccination) and Nigerian (screening) evidence, and to state that the sensitivity results should be read as robustness checks rather than as an implicit claim of insensitivity to the assumption.

**Manuscript change**: (i) A new Methods paragraph under §Scenarios labels the paired-OR design explicitly as the education-uptake sensitivity analysis; (ii) a signpost sentence added to the Results paragraph on screening scale-up names the paired-OR contrast rather than reporting it as an inline aside; (iii) an extended OR sweep at intermediate values (vax_OR ∈ {2, 3, 8}; screening_OR ∈ {2, 5}) has been added to the scenario matrix, with results shown in new Supplementary Figure S4 (cancers averted vs. OR value, one panel per gradient) and Supplementary Table S3 (numeric results per OR value).

**Comment 3**: Please expand the description of model calibration and goodness-of-fit, including how the best-fitting parameter sets were selected and how calibration uncertainty was incorporated.

**Response**: The previous Methods description of the Nigeria calibration relied on cross-references to previous applications (refs 15, 21, 16) for the algorithmic details. We expanded the Methods "Model overview and Nigeria calibration" subsection to cover:

- **Calibration algorithm**: HPVsim's built-in `hpv.Calibration` class (v3.1.0), an Optuna-driven parameter sampler. We ran 1000 trials across 100 parallel workers on a multi-core VM. Each trial samples a candidate parameter set (14 free parameters spanning per-act transmission, casual/marital layer mixing, cross-immunity, and per-genotype natural-history parameters — full list in Table S1) from within uniform priors, runs the simulation, and returns a weighted goodness-of-fit score against the five target series.
- **Fitting targets and weighting**: Age-specific new cervical cancer cases (Globocan), age-standardized cancer incidence (WHO 2000 standard), HPV genotype distribution in cancer, HPV genotype distribution in high-grade lesions (CIN), and age-specific HPV DNA prevalence in women aged 15–49 (HPV Information Centre meta-analyses). Weights across the five targets are inherited from the previous applications, balancing the number of data points per target so that no single high-density series dominates the objective.
- **Top-set selection**: Trials are ranked by their weighted mismatch score after all 1000 trials complete. The top 100 are retained in a shrunken calibration object committed to the repository; the top 50 are used to report parameter ranges in Table S1 and to overlay against the calibration targets in Figure S3; 10 top-ranked draws are propagated through the scenario runs (below).
- **Propagation of calibration uncertainty**: Each of the 10 top-ranked parameter draws is run across each scenario at 5 different model seeds, producing 50 replicates per scenario. Reported point estimates are the median across these 50 replicates; uncertainty ranges are the 25th–75th percentile IQR and, in this revision, the 2.5–97.5 percentile 95% UI (see Comment 4). This design captures calibration uncertainty (across the 10 parameter draws) and stochastic uncertainty (across the 5 seeds within each draw) jointly in the reported bands.

Goodness of fit is shown in Figure S2 (age-specific HPV prevalence, cancer cases, ASR, and genotype distributions overlaid on calibration targets) and Figure S3 (top-50 time series for HPV prevalence, CIN prevalence, and ASR). We revised the captions of both figures to make their role as goodness-of-fit diagnostics explicit, and added a short subsection to Methods labelling Figure S2 as the primary goodness-of-fit check.

**Manuscript change**: Methods §Model overview and Nigeria calibration expanded per above; Table S1 caption revised to note the top-50 selection cutoff; Figures S2 and S3 captions revised to frame them explicitly as goodness-of-fit diagnostics; new Methods subsection added labelling Figure S2 as the primary goodness-of-fit check.

**Response**: We agree. **Comment 5**: Please provide additional justification for the assumptions underpinning screening scale-up to 70% coverage and discuss the practical feasibility of achieving this level of uptake in Nigeria.

**Response**: The 70% lifetime coverage target is drawn directly from the WHO Global Strategy to Accelerate the Elimination of Cervical Cancer as a Public Health Problem (WHO, 2020; new ref XX), which sets "70% of women screened with a high-performance test by age 35, and again by age 45" as the middle pillar of the 90-70-90 elimination targets. The WHO-commissioned modelling analysis that underpinned this target (Brisson et al. 2020, Lancet; new ref XX) included Nigeria in its 78-country analysis and used the same 70% figure. We revised the Methods §Scenarios paragraph to attribute the target explicitly to this framework rather than presenting it as a self-standing assumption.

On practical feasibility, we agree the target is aspirational for Nigeria given current opportunistic coverage of roughly 10–15% (ref 28), and we now make explicit that our scenario models the target rather than a forecast. The Discussion's "Programmatic feasibility" subsection was expanded with a paragraph naming (i) a large-scale sub-Saharan African screen-and-treat program as an empirical anchor for what population-level scale-up looks like in a comparable setting (Parham et al. 2015, PLOS ONE, Zambia; new ref XX — DOI to verify), and (ii) a Nigeria-specific analysis of cervical screening barriers to name the specific constraints Nigeria faces [REF PENDING — the automated literature search returned candidate authors (Anorlu, Morhason-Bello, Ndikom) but could not verify a specific citation; a suitable reference will be selected from the author group's own reading before submission]. The paragraph makes clear that (a) the 70% scenario reflects the WHO target rather than an empirical projection, (b) achieving it in Nigeria would require substantial investment in cervical screening infrastructure, and (c) results reported under this scenario should be read as the upper bound on what screening scale-up can contribute over the study horizon.

**Manuscript change**: Methods §Scenarios revised to attribute the 70% screening target explicitly to WHO 2020 and the Brisson et al. 2020 modelling analysis (two new references added to the bibliography); Discussion "Programmatic feasibility" subsection extended with a paragraph on the gap between current Nigerian screening coverage and the WHO target, referencing a regional feasibility anchor (Parham et al. 2015) and a Nigeria-specific screening-barriers citation (to be finalized).

**Comment 6**: Please clarify the extent to which other important determinants of vaccine and screening access (such as wealth, geography, and healthcare access) may influence outcomes beyond the education-related effects incorporated in the model.

**Response**: We agree that wealth, geography (particularly the north-south gradient in Nigeria), and healthcare access are important determinants of both HPV vaccine uptake and cervical screening access, and that our education-only gradient is a partial proxy for a broader composite gradient of disadvantage. We modelled education explicitly because a well-calibrated Nigeria-specific data point exists for its effect on screening uptake (ref 33) and a defensible regional proxy exists for its effect on vaccination (Malawi/Ethiopia campaign data, refs 29–30). Equivalent quantitative estimates of wealth-, geography-, or healthcare-access-linked HPV vaccine or screening uptake in Nigeria at the level of precision needed to parameterize an additional independent gradient were not available to us; the closest available Nigerian evidence is the general childhood-vaccination equity data already cited (Gavi 2023 zero-dose analysis, ref 27; 2018 DHS gradients by maternal education, ref 32), plus a further Nigerian equity-decomposition analysis added in this revision [REF PENDING — automated search returned candidate directions (Adedokun, Antai, Bobo, Wollum) but could not verify a specific citation without web access; a suitable reference will be selected from the author group's own reading before submission].

The critical point for interpretation is that education, wealth, geography, and healthcare access are not independent determinants in Nigeria — they are strongly co-linear predictors of health service use, so the joint gradient along any composite index of disadvantage is very likely steeper than the education-only gradient we modelled. Our equity result — that residual cervical cancer burden concentrates on educationally-disadvantaged birth cohorts, and that infant delivery does not automatically resolve this — should therefore be read as a lower bound on the true equity concern, not an upper bound. A model with the true joint gradient would produce a larger residual burden and a larger equity gap, not a smaller one. We added a paragraph to the Discussion §Limitations making this argument explicit, naming the north-south gradient in cervical screening infrastructure as a specific residual gradient that is unmodelled but would likely amplify the effect we do model.

**Manuscript change**: Discussion §Limitations extended with a paragraph on the reduction of a multivariate equity gradient to an education-only OR, its co-linearity with wealth/geography/access, and the implication that the reported equity gap is a lower bound; references added for a Nigerian wealth-gradient or geographic-heterogeneity analysis (to be finalized).

**Comment 7**: Please further moderate policy-oriented conclusions regarding infant HPV vaccination and emphasise that the findings are exploratory, hypothesis-generating, and dependent on currently unverified assumptions regarding infant immunogenicity, durability, and programme implementation.

**Response**: We agree that the appropriate register for the infant-delivery portion of this analysis is exploratory and hypothesis-generating, given that its key inputs on infant HPV vaccine performance are not yet empirically established. The previous revision carried this framing distributed across several hedges (e.g. Abstract Conclusions "may have potential to circumvent"; Discussion opener "could in principle"; the Discussion "biological uncertainty" and "programmatic feasibility" subsections both list the unverified assumptions on which any positive infant-delivery finding rests). In this revision we made the exploratory framing explicit and prominent rather than distributed. Specifically:

- The Abstract Conclusions now labels the infant-delivery findings explicitly as exploratory and hypothesis-generating, and names the three unverified empirical prerequisites (infant immunogenicity in the first months of life, durability across the ~15–25-year interval to peak HPV exposure, and coverage achievable through Nigeria's routine childhood immunization platform) as the conditions any policy decision would depend on.
- The Discussion opening paragraph now flags early that all infant-delivery findings should be read as conditional on assumptions that current evidence cannot verify, anchored to the two specific citation gaps (no HPV vaccine trial has enrolled anyone below age 10; no follow-up extends past ~11 years).
- The Discussion closing paragraph now states directly that this analysis is not a basis for a policy recommendation contingent on infant HPV delivery, and that the sustained-and-equitably-scaled adolescent program plus population-based screening — both of which rest on empirically-supported parameters — is the actionable near-term recommendation.

We preserved the different evidentiary standing of the adolescent-delivery and screening findings, which rest on parameters calibrated to Nigerian data or drawn from published trials in comparable settings; recasting these as exploratory alongside the infant findings would obscure the distinction the reviewer's comment implicitly draws.

**Manuscript change**: Abstract Conclusions rewritten to label infant-delivery findings explicitly as exploratory and to name the three empirical prerequisites; Discussion opening paragraph and closing paragraph amended to signpost this framing; care taken to preserve the distinct evidentiary standing of the adolescent-delivery and screening findings.
